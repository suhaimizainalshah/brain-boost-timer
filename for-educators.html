For educators: the research behind the breaks

Brain Boost Time(r) helps you plan purposeful breaks: moving, learning while moving, recalling and resting. Here is what the classroom research supports, and what it doesn’t.

🏅 Gold, Innergy Award 2022 · Ministry of Education, Singapore

Four kinds of break

The original project found that breaks work in more than one way: not only rest, but movement, and breaks that keep the lesson going. The timer’s Break ideas follow the same four kinds.

MoveShort bursts of activity, such as stretching or jogging on the spot.
Learn and moveMovement with lesson content, such as four corners or true-or-false stand or sit.
RecallSeated retrieval, such as a brain dump or turn and teach.
RestA calm minute: breathing, or looking far away.

What the evidence shows

FindingWhat it means for your lessonSource
Classroom physical activity improved on-task and reduced off-task behaviour (a moderate effect in a meta-analysis).Movement breaks are worth the time they take.Watson et al., 2017
Short active breaks that include curriculum content are a recognised form of classroom activity.Learn-and-move breaks can revise content while students move.Watson et al., 2017
Behaviour improved after longer moderate breaks (over 10 minutes) or shorter, more intense ones (about 5 minutes).Make short breaks brisk; gentle breaks need longer.Daly-Smith et al., 2018
Breaks of 10 minutes or less gave small boosts to energy and reduced fatigue; longer breaks helped performance more.Rest breaks help students feel ready; don’t expect them to raise scores on their own.Albulescu et al., 2022
Practice testing and spacing out study are the two highest-utility learning techniques; rereading and highlighting are not.A recall break, such as a brain dump, is also good revision.Dunlosky et al., 2013

Be careful with bigger claims. There isn’t yet consistent evidence that movement breaks improve cognition or test scores, because too few studies have been repeated (Daly-Smith et al., 2018). And teachers interested in neuroscience often believe neuromyths, particularly those behind commercial programmes such as Brain Gym and “learning styles” (Dekker et al., 2012), so Brain Boost Time(r) doesn’t include them.

Try it in a lesson

This 45-minute plan puts a learn-and-move break after the teaching and a recall break before packing up. It opens in the timer, where you can change any step and save it.

  1. Do now: 5 min
  2. Teach: 15 min
  3. Four corners (learn and move): 5 min
  4. Practice: 15 min
  5. Brain dump (recall): 3 min
  6. Pack up: 2 min

Making breaks work for every student

  • Offer a seated option for any movement break, so students with mobility needs or injuries can join in.
  • Keep it safe: clear bags from aisles, and use space-light ideas in small rooms.
  • Mind sensory needs: a calm break may suit some students better after an active one.
  • See what works for your class: watch how settled students are in the ten minutes after a break, and try a kind of break for two weeks before judging it.
